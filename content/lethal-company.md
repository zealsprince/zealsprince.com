# Three and a half million downloads

Lethal Company is the game I have modded most, and by reach it is comfortably the widest-travelled work I have done. Two mods, both still maintained, with more than 3.4 million downloads between them.

Both exist for the same reason. Lethal Company's core loop is excellent and it settles. Once a crew knows the maps and the monsters, the game stops surprising them, and the tension that made it worth playing drains out. These two put pressure back without arguing with the rules the game already taught you.

## Locker

**2.4 million downloads.** A new enemy that responds to what players can and cannot see.

It borrows the instinct behind the coilhead, where looking is the mechanic, then does something else with it. The goal was situational rather than constant. It is not another thing patrolling the halls padding out the encounter table, it is something that turns a corridor you had already solved back into a decision.

For a new player it is a scare. For an experienced crew it is the moment the map stops being memorised.

The part I did not expect is how many community modpacks depend on it. Plenty of people have run into Locker without ever knowing whose mod it was, which is about the best outcome a mod can have.

## Malfunctions

**1 million downloads.** Random ship failures, and a way to deal with them.

The point was never damage for its own sake. Each malfunction takes something the crew relies on and removes it, then hands back a route to recovery, so the day becomes a problem to solve rather than a run to write off.

- **Lockdown** seals the door until 10pm with the controls disabled, so your usual retreat is gone.
- **Navigation** waits until the ship is back in orbit, overwrites the routing commands and steers you to a random moon for free, with no read on the moon or its weather.

When a malfunction is scheduled for the day, a repair bot is guaranteed to spawn in the dungeon. Bring it back and it clears everything.

That guarantee is the whole design. It turns a malfunction into an objective instead of a punishment, and it gives the crew a reason to go somewhere they otherwise would not have gone.

## What modding is good for

Modding is design against constraints you did not choose, which is a genuinely different exercise to building from nothing. The game has an existing vocabulary and players have built expectations on top of it. A good mod extends that vocabulary. A bad one argues with it.

It is also the fastest feedback loop I work in. A change ships and thousands of people hit it the same week, which is a very direct way to find out whether an idea was as good as it seemed.

Most of both version histories is tuning rather than features. How often should this matter? Too frequent and players route around it until it is background noise. Too rare and nobody meets it at all. Anything touching the ship has the extra constraint of hitting every player at once, so a failure has to read clearly to someone who was not watching when it fired.

I also maintain the Vanilla Enhanced and Extended modpacks for people who would rather not assemble a mod list themselves.
